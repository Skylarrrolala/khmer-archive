# 1. នំកង — Num Kong

## 1.1 Identity & naming

- **Khmer spelling:** នំកង is confirmed. Cambodianess (2026) prints "num kang (នំកង)" under a photo taken in Phnom Penh. Neither Headley nor Chuon Nath has នំកង as a headword.
- **Recommended transliteration:** keep **Num Kong**. Alternatives:
  - *num kang* (Cambodianess)
  - *noum kong* (Cambodge Mag)
  - *nom korng* (Vegan Food Quest)
  - *num korng* (the archive's own filename)

  All of these approximate Headley's pronunciation /kɑɑŋ/.
- **Meaning:** in Khmer, កង means "ring, circle; bracelet, necklace, anklet" (Headley 1997). Chuon Nath defines it as a round ornament worn on the body, e.g. កងដៃ (bracelet). The cakes are ring-shaped. However, **no source consulted says the dessert is named for this meaning**, so present it as the meaning of the word, not as the etymology of the name.
- **Spelling caution:** Cambodge Mag glosses the cake as "bicycle wheel cake". "Wheel/bicycle" is the meaning of កង់ (with a final ់), not កង (Headley 1997). This suggests some writers read the name as នំកង់. That reading is unresolved. The Cambodianess spelling នំកង is the only Khmer-script attestation found.
- **Do not confuse with:**
  - នំប៉ោង (num paong): hollow sesame doughnuts (Cambodianess, 3 Nov 2026).
  - នំគម (num kom): a pyramidal cake (Headley).

## 1.2 Photo correspondence

The photo is plausibly num kong. It shows golden, ring-shaped fried dough with a glossy amber glaze and dense white sesame. This matches Cambodianess: "golden, glossy… sesame… ring-shaped… honey-colored sheen".

The rings sit on a woven rattan tray over long, blade-like green leaves (banana or similar). The leaves are **not lotus**: lotus leaves are round. The image is a styled food photograph of unknown origin, so its provenance still needs confirming under the project's photo rule.

## 1.5 Proposed narrative

Num kong is a Cambodian fried ring cake. A dough of glutinous rice flour, sometimes with a little ordinary rice flour and usually bound with coconut milk, is rolled into ropes, looped into rings and deep-fried until golden. The rings are then coated in palm sugar cooked down to a dark, sticky caramel and scattered with sesame seeds, which gives them their glossy, honey-coloured finish.

In Khmer, kang is the everyday word for a ring or bracelet, and the cakes are ring-shaped, although no published source examined explains the name directly. In English the cake is also written num kang, noum kong and nom korng.

Num kong is an everyday sweet rather than a festival dish. Published accounts describe it as a street dessert, sold from stalls and by vendors in Phnom Penh. One recent account describes the cake as having Chinese roots: an example of Cambodian cooking taking in a neighbouring tradition and making it its own.

## 1.6 Proposed occasion line

Everyday street and market snack. No festival or ceremonial use is documented.

## 1.7 Unresolved

- **Regions.** "Siem Reap, Kampong Chhnang, market towns" needs a regional food survey, provincial reporting, or the family's own testimony recorded in `description`.
- **Late-afternoon selling and lotus-leaf wrapping.** No evidence was found. Settling these needs observation, a vendor interview, or published market reporting.
- **"Rice rather than wheat" framing.** This is unsupported, and the one source on origins points to Chinese influence instead. Settling it needs food-history scholarship on Khmer fried doughs.
- **Chinese origin.** Only one short source (K1) makes this claim. A second, independent food-history source would settle it.
- **Name etymology.** Is the name from កង (ring) or written នំកង់ (wheel)? A Khmer-language source (cookbook, Khmer news, a Ministry of Culture publication) using either spelling and explaining it would settle this.
- **Weddings and ceremonies.** Nothing credible was found either way.
- **Dough liquid.** Water versus coconut milk: the recipes found both use coconut milk. A home or vendor source would show whether water-based dough is also traditional.

# 2. នំកោ — "Num Kor" (recommended: នំអាកោត្នោត, Num Akor Thnot)

## 2.1 Identity & naming

**Recommendation: rename the entry to នំអាកោត្នោត (Num Akor Thnot), "steamed palm-fruit rice cake".** The generic cake is នំអាកោ (num akor). ត្នោត (thnot, the sugar palm) marks the palm-fruit variant that the text and photo describe. The image filename `num-akor.jpg` already uses the correct name.

Evidence for the recommendation:
- Chuon Nath has the headword អាកោ: "a kind of steamed cake" leavened and steamed in cups. Under the same entry, it defines **អាកោត្នោត** as akor made by mixing ripe palm-fruit flesh with flour and sugar. This is exactly the dessert the archive describes.
- Headley 1997 has the headword នំអាកោ, defined as a steamed cake "made of rice flour, yeast, sugar, coconut steamed in small cups called ពែងអាកោ".
- Le Petit Journal (Médeville, 2022) names the palm-fruit cake "akao tnaot (អាកោត្នោត)".
- **នំកោ is not attested.** There is no headword នំកោ in Chuon Nath, Headley 1977 or Headley 1997 (SEAlang search: "Nothing found"). Headley lists កោ only as a cross-reference to កុរ, which is unrelated. No published use of នំកោ for this cake was found. The likeliest explanation is a truncation of អាកោ (dropping អា).

Spelling notes:
- Chuon Nath and Headley both spell **អាកោ**, without a final រ. Wikipedia spells it នំអាកោរ; that form returned no dictionary entry. Use អាកោ.
- Romanisations:
  - *num akor* (Wikipedia)
  - *nom akor* (Vegan Food Quest)
  - *akao tnaot* (Le Petit Journal)
  - *nom akor t'nau* (Vegan Food Quest)
  - Headley's pronunciation is /ʔaakao/.

  "Num Akor Thnot" is the recommended form, matching the existing filename.
- Headley 1977 glosses អាកោ as a "baked cake". Headley 1997 and Chuon Nath both say steamed. Treat "steamed" as correct.
- Curatorial note: Headley 1997 also records a vulgar slang sense of នំអាកោ. The full name with ត្នោត keeps the label unambiguous.
- **Do not confuse with:**
  - នំគម (num kom, also written "num korm"): a pyramidal cake with a coconut filling (Headley). This is the other plausible source of the "kor" spelling.
  - num tnaot: a separate palm cake "made with rice grains" (Le Petit Journal).

## 2.2 Photo correspondence

The photo plausibly shows num akor thnot. It has orange-yellow domed cakes in folded banana-leaf cups, each heaped with white shredded coconut. This matches:
- Chuon Nath: leavened cake steamed in cups, made with ripe palm flesh.
- Le Petit Journal: light-orange batter in folded banana-leaf cones that swell during steaming.
- Vegan Food Quest (weak): yellow palm-fruit variety topped with fresh shredded coconut.

The image alone cannot show where or by whom the cakes were made. Visually similar palm-fruit cakes may be made in neighbouring countries; this is unverified and was not researched. Provenance is unknown.

## 2.5 Proposed narrative

Num akor thnot is the palm-fruit form of num akor, a small Cambodian steamed cake. The Khmer dictionary of Chuon Nath defines akor as a cake of flour and sugar, leavened and steamed in cups so that it rises light and soft. It defines akor thnot as the version made by mixing the flesh of ripe sugar-palm fruit into the flour and sugar.

The sugar palm, Borassus flabellifer, was named Cambodia's national tree by royal decree in 2005. For this cake the fruit is left until its skin turns dark brown and orange, and the fibrous orange pulp is scraped free. It is worked into a batter of rice flour, coconut cream and yeast. The light-orange batter is spooned into small cups folded from banana leaf and steamed slowly, swelling as it cooks, and is often finished with coconut cream or grated coconut.

Because it depends on ripe fruit, the cake follows the fruit season, reported as May to October. That is a different season from the dry-season tapping of palm sap for sugar, which runs from about December to May.

Steamed palm cakes are sold by street vendors and in markets, including at Oudong in Kampong Speu province, and have been ordered in quantity for weddings and ceremonies.

## 2.6 Proposed occasion line

Market and street sweet, made in the palm-fruit season (reported May to October). Steamed palm cakes are also reported as ordered in quantity for weddings and ceremonies.

## 2.7 Unresolved

- **Sweetener.** Is it specifically palm sugar? The dictionaries say only "sugar", and only Wikipedia says palm sugar. A Khmer recipe source or vendor interview would settle it.
- **"Fermented rice starter".** Is the leaven a rice starter (មេ, e.g. dried yeast cake) or commercial yeast? Settling it needs a Khmer cookery source.
- **Salted shredded coconut topping.** Shredded coconut is weakly attested. "Salted" is unattested.
- **Takeo as a "heartland".** Takeo is not in the GI and has only generic palm-province mentions. A Ministry of Agriculture or Commerce production survey would settle it.
- **A4's cakes.** A4 says "steamed palm cakes" without a Khmer name. Confirming they are akor thnot would need the original ThmeyThmey/Khmer-language report or an interview.
- **Spelling of the final consonant.** អាកោរ (Wikipedia) versus អាកោ (dictionaries) is open. A post-1990s Ministry of Education spelling reference would settle it.
- **Exact fruit season.** May–October comes from one report. It may vary by province. Agricultural extension data on Borassus fruiting would settle it.
- **Photo.** Its origin is unverified.

Summary:
1. "នំកោ / Num Kor" is not attested in Chuon Nath or Headley. The described cake is **នំអាកោត្នោត (Num Akor Thnot)**, defined in Chuon Nath as akor made with ripe sugar-palm flesh. Rename the entry; the photo filename already says "akor".
2. Borassus flabellifer as national tree is confirmed by Royal Decree NS/RKT/0305/149 (21 Mar 2005). Kampong Speu palm sugar is a confirmed GI covering Oudong and Samrong Tong plus Ang Snuol in Kandal; Takeo is outside the GI area.
3. The Num Kor "dry season" claim is contradicted. Palm sap runs Dec–May, but palm fruit ripens May–Oct, so the fruit cake follows the fruit season.
4. នំកង is attested (Cambodianess). The core of the entry (glutinous rice flour, ring shape, fried, palm-sugar caramel, sesame, everyday street sweet) is supported. The regions, late-afternoon selling, lotus-leaf wrapping, warm-water dough and "rice rather than wheat" framing are unsupported; one source says Chinese roots.
5. Both photos plausibly match their desserts, but neither photo's provenance is established, and Num Kong evidence is thin (one short news feature plus recipes).
