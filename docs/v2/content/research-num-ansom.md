# Fact-check: Num Ansom Chek and Num Ansom Chrouk

Prepared 2026-11-21 for "Khmer Living Archive — The Cambodian Sweets Collection".
Checked against `khmer-archive-national-museum-redesign/supabase/seed.sql` (the rows for នំអន្សមចេក and នំអន្សមជ្រូក). No repository file was edited.

Status key: SUPPORTED = a credible source states it · PARTLY = part of it is sourced, or only weak sources support it · CONTRADICTED = sources say something different · UNSUPPORTED = no source found.

Reference numbers such as [R1] point to the shared reference list in section 3, which covers both desserts.

**Headline finding on cooking.** The Chuon Nath dictionary defines អន្សម as cooked by **boiling (ស្ងោរ) or grilling (អាំង)**. Its entry for num korm says that one is **steamed (ចំហុយ)**, so the dictionary separates the two verbs on purpose. English-language press usually writes "steamed". The Vice report explains why: the wrapped logs go into a pot of boiling water, and the leaf wrapping keeps the water out, so the rice cooks in its own steam. In the archive, "boiled" is the accurate verb. Use it in both places in place of the current steamed/boiled contradiction.

## A. នំអន្សមចេក — Num Ansom Chek

### 1. Identity & naming

- **Khmer spelling, verified:** នំអន្សមចេក. The headword អន្សម appears in the Chuon Nath dictionary [R1], which lists អន្សមចេក as a named variety. Wikipedia gives the same script [R10]. ចេក = banana.
- **Romanisation:** Wikipedia gives *num ânsâm* (formal romanisation, close to UNGEGN) [R10].
- **Recommended transliteration:** **Num Ansom Chek.** "Ansom" is the form used in the Wikipedia article title [R10], in Cambodianess (2023, 2026) [R2][R3] and in the Phnom Penh Post (2022) [R6].
- **Alternative spellings in credible use:**
  - *ansorm*: Phnom Penh Post 2017 [R4], Guinness record title (as reported) [R9], IntoCambodia [R12].
  - *nom ansom / nom ansorm*: Phnom Penh Post 2021 [R5].
  - *onsom*: used in an RFA Khmer URL. That article was not opened.
- **Note:** the archive currently writes "Ansorm" in `name_english` but "ansom" in photo filenames. Either spelling is defensible; use one consistently.
- **Banana:** the dictionary specifies a whole ripe **chek nam va** (ចេកណាំវ៉ា) [R1]. Wikipedia glosses chek nam va as the "Thai banana" [R11], and IntoCambodia calls it Pisang Awak [R12]. The current text's "baby bananas" is a mistranslation.

### 4. Proposed narrative

Num ansom chek is a cylinder of glutinous rice wrapped in banana leaf around a whole ripe chek nam va, the Thai banana. The Chuon Nath Khmer dictionary defines ansom as a cake of glutinous rice in fresh leaves, usually banana, round and even at both ends, cooked by boiling or grilling. It lists the banana version first.

The tightly bound logs are cooked for several hours in a large pot of boiling water. The leaf keeps the water out, so the rice cooks in its own steam. The work takes time and many hands. The same dictionary records the saying "ten people, and the ansom burns" for a crowd that still cannot get a job done.

Num ansom is closely tied to Pchum Ben. In the final days before Pchum Ben day, families gather to wrap the cakes as offerings for Buddhist monks, made on behalf of departed relatives. It is also made for Khmer New Year and for weddings. A historian and a Ministry of Culture official quoted in the Cambodian press link the cylindrical ansom, and its pyramid-shaped partner num korm, to the Hindu god Shiva and his consort, and describe the pair as honouring mother and father.

Today many city families buy the cakes, or receive them from relatives in the countryside.

### 5. Proposed occasion line

Pchum Ben · Khmer New Year · Weddings

### 4. Proposed narrative

Num ansom chrouk is the savoury form of num ansom: glutinous rice and mung beans wrapped in banana leaf around a core of pork, and bound into a cylinder. The Chuon Nath Khmer dictionary lists it among the kinds of ansom, defining it simply as ansom with pork at its centre. It appears alongside the banana-filled ansom chek.

Its place in a collection of sweets comes from Khmer usage rather than taste. Num, the word that begins its name, is the dictionary's term for cakes made from rice or flour, moist or dry. The dictionary counts them among snacks and sweets of every kind. Cambodian reporting describes num ansom as made with either savoury or sweet fillings.

Like the banana version, the logs are cooked for several hours in a pot of boiling water. The pork cake is wrapped for Pchum Ben, when cakes are offered to monks on behalf of the dead. It is also made for other festivals, including Khmer New Year. Slices are sometimes fried and eaten with fish sauce and pickled vegetables.

It is made in other provinces too. In 2021, officials in Stung Treng described their local recipe as a hallmark of the province.

### 7. Collection-fit note

**Recommendation: keep the entry, and frame it explicitly as a savoury num.**

- **Why the Khmer category covers it.** The collection's Khmer category is នំ (*num*). Chuon Nath defines នំ as food "made from rice or flour" that is "counted among foods or sweets of every kind" (R1: "រាប់ចូលក្នុងពួកចំណីឬបង្អែមគ្រប់មុខ"). So in Khmer, num is wider than "dessert".
- **Ansom is one dish with both fillings.** The dictionary lists sweet and savoury ansom under one headword (R1). Cambodianess describes num ansom as coming "with either savory or sweet fillings" (R2). Num ansom chrouk is therefore a variety of the same cake as ansom chek, not a separate savoury dish.
- **Suggested presentation:**
  - Keep "savoury" in the English subtitle. Its absence would mislead a reader of a "Sweets Collection".
  - Pair or cross-reference the entry with Num Ansom Chek.
  - Add one line to the collection introduction saying that the collection follows the Khmer category num, which includes some savoury cakes.
- **The alternative.** If the founder wants the collection to be strictly sweet, this entry does not fit, and should be dropped rather than relabelled.
