# Fact-check: Num Plae Ai and Num Kroch

Prepared 2026-11-21. Every source listed under References was opened on that date. Khmer quotes are copied from the source with the zero-width spaces removed. English glosses in square brackets are this report's translations, not the source's own wording.

How to read the dictionary citations: the SEAlang Library Khmer Dictionary is an online database that holds three printed dictionaries. They are Chuon Nath's *Khmer Dictionary* (Buddhist Institute, Phnom Penh; SEAlang dates it to 1966, and its data id is `chuon67`), Headley et al. 1977, and Headley, Chim & Soeum 1997. Each result shows which of the three it comes from, so every entry cited below is from a named print dictionary.

# 1. Num Plae Ai — នំផ្លែអាយ

## 1.1 Identity & naming

- **Khmer spelling (verified):** នំផ្លែអាយ. Chuon Nath, Headley 1977 and Headley 1997 all have it as a headword.
- **Pronunciation (Headley, IPA):** num plae ʔaay
- **Recommended transliteration:** *num plae ai*. It matches the IPA above and is the title of the English Wikipedia article.
- **Alternative romanisations seen in opened sources:** nom plae ai (196 Flavors), nom plai ai (Kroya), num plae ay / num plae aiy / nom plae ay / nom plaiy aiy (Wikipedia). The spelling *num plea ai* did not appear in any source opened.
- **Other Khmer names for the same sweet (strong; Chuon Nath and Headley 1997):**
  - **នំបំពួនស្ករ** *num bompuon skor* (IPA bɑmpuən skɑɑ). Literally "cake that hides the sugar": បំពួន means "to hide, to cover up" (Headley 1997). Chuon Nath calls it the original name.
  - **នំកើតក្ដី** *num kaet kdei* (IPA kaət kdəy). Literally "lawsuit cake": កើតក្តី means "to be involved in a lawsuit" (Headley 1977). Chuon Nath calls it colloquial and rarely used.
- **What the name means (sourced):**
  - នំ means cake or dessert (Headley 1977, p. 457, as quoted on Wikipedia).
  - ផ្លែ means fruit (Headley 1977, p. 622, as quoted on Wikipedia).
  - អាយ means "here, over here, on this side" and also "local, near" (Headley 1977 and 1997; Chuon Nath).
  - Chuon Nath gives the explanation "elders relate" (ចាស់ៗតំណាលប្រាប់ថា). In the reign of King Ang Duong, the cakes were fixed in rows on branches pushed into a section of banana trunk, so that they looked like a fruit tree. They were therefore called "fruit **here** (អាយ)", meaning fruit from a tree close at hand rather than fruit picked from a tree **over there** (នាយ). This is a traditional explanation recorded by an authoritative dictionary, not a documented history.
  - Identifying the king: Chuon Nath names him ព្រះបាទសម្ដេចព្រះហរិរក្សរាមាឥស្សរាធិបតី. That is Ang Duong's throne title (English Wikipedia, "Ang Duong"; he reigned from 1841 or 1848 to 1860).
- **Weakly attested English renderings:**
  - "rice sugar pearls" (Rivière 2008) and "sweet rice dumplings" (De Monteiro & Neustadt 1998). Wikipedia cites both, but the books were not opened.
  - "cakes of forgiveness" (Kroya). This should not be used: the same page writes the Khmer as "ណម ផ្លៃអៃ", which is a garbled back-transliteration and not the real name.

**Note on the folk name.** All the opened sources for "num samlap pdei" are English-language recipe or travel pages: 196 Flavors (2018), a blogspot page that copies it, and Indochina Tour (2026).
- 196 Flavors itself calls the story "an interesting speculation" taken from "Khmer fables".
- In that version the husband **chokes** on hot, sticky cakes. It does not say that hot sugar squirts out.
- Searches in Khmer for "នំសម្លាប់ប្ដី" and "នំផ្លែអាយ សម្លាប់ប្ដី" found nothing, and neither the Chuon Nath nor the Headley dictionary has the name.

The "hot syrup bursts out" folk explanation **is** attested, in Chuon Nath, but for a different name: **num kaet kdei**, the "lawsuit cake". In that tale, syrup from a half-bitten cake sprays another diner's face, a fight follows, and a judge names the cake. The seed text appears to have merged the blog nickname with the dictionary's folk tale. If the archive wants a folk name, *num kaet kdei* has the stronger source.

## 1.4 Proposed narrative

Num plae ai (នំផ្លែអាយ) is a small Khmer sweet of glutinous rice dough wrapped around a lump of palm sugar. The Khmer dictionary of Chuon Nath, published by the Buddhist Institute, describes it as a fresh cake in which the sugar is hidden from sight. It gives the sweet three names: num bompuon skor, the cake that hides the sugar, which it calls the original and most literal name; num plae ai; and, less commonly, num kaet kdei.

The dictionary also records explanations handed down by elders. Num plae ai, roughly the fruit of this side, is said to recall a custom in the reign of King Ang Duong, in the nineteenth century, when the cakes were fixed in rows on branches set into a piece of banana trunk, so that they looked like a fruiting tree close at hand. Num kaet kdei, the cake that caused a lawsuit, comes from a tale in which syrup spurted from a half-bitten cake into another man's face, starting a quarrel that ended before a judge.

In recipes published today, the filled balls are boiled, cooled and rolled in grated coconut. Food writers often describe num plae ai as an everyday snack, made at home and sold by street vendors.

*(Word count 207 (Khmer name counted as one word). The "husband-killing cake" nickname is deliberately left out; see 1.2.)*

## 1.6 Unresolved — Num Plae Ai

- **Any festival link (Pchum Ben, Khmer New Year, weddings).** These appear only in unsourced or unopened listicles. Settling it needs Cambodian ethnographic writing, a Ministry of Culture and Fine Arts publication, or Khmer-language press describing the sweet at a named festival.
- **Regions (Takeo; "nationwide").** No source covers either. A regional food survey or provincial press coverage would settle it.
- **"Num samlap pdei".** It is found only on English-language blogs since 2018. Settling it would need a Khmer-language print or press source using នំសម្លាប់ប្ដី for this sweet; none was found.
- **The Ang Duong custom.** Chuon Nath records it as what elders relate. No independent historical source was found for the display of cakes on branches.
- **The cookbooks Wikipedia cites** (Rivière 2008, p. 91; De Monteiro & Neustadt 1998, pp. 274–275; Ros & Lee 2019, p. 209). Not opened. They should be checked for the boiling method and the serving details (banana-leaf cups).
- **Salt in the coconut, and texture words.** Not supported. Leave them out unless a family source gives them.

# 2. Num Kroch — នំក្រូច

(This text belongs to the contributor and is not rewritten. Only identity, a claim check and references are given.)

## 2.1 Identity & naming

- **Khmer spelling (verified):** នំក្រូច. It is a headword in Headley 1997, and Cambodianess uses the same spelling.
- **Pronunciation (Headley 1997, IPA):** num krooc
- **Recommended transliteration:** *num kroch*. Alternative: *nom kroch* (196 Flavors, Cup of Yum). "Nom krouch" appears only in low-quality search hits and is not cited.
- **Other name:** Khmer Wikipedia also gives នំគ្រាប់ល្ង ("sesame-seed cake"), but that article is a machine-style translation of the Chinese *jian dui* article and is **weak**.
- **Meaning:** ក្រូច = "citrus fruit, orange" (Headley 1997; Chuon Nath defines ក្រូច as a group of citrus trees). That the cake is **named for its round, orange-like shape** is stated only by 196 Flavors (weak), and Wikipedia's *Jian dui* article repeats it citing that same page. Treat that explanation as weakly attested.

## 2.4 Unresolved — Num Kroch

- **The shape etymology ("named because it looks like an orange").** It rests on one recipe blog. A Khmer-language source, or a dictionary note in Chuon Nath or a later Khmer dictionary, would settle it.
- **How and when it reached Cambodia from Chinese *jian dui*.** It is described only as a "connection" (Cambodianess) and with a hedged "is said to have originated" (196 Flavors). Settling it needs food-history writing on Sino-Khmer cuisine.
- **A coconut filling.** No source mentions one for the Cambodian sweet.
- **Its status as a traditional everyday snack, and any occasions.** Only blogs comment on this.
